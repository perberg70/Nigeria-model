How to read these numbers

Together the three effects produce a net change of +4.3 index points — 288 deaths a year avoided, the figure in the green tile. A high-electrification pathway delivers the strongest health gains when the additional electricity is also low-emission.

The largest driver is not on any slider: bioenergy is 2.5% of Colombia's generation but 73% of its power-sector fine particulates, so which low-emission sources grow matters more here than how much low-emission generation there is.

How these health figures are worked out
  • 1How much the emissions change. Two ratios against 2024: the power sector's particulate emissions, which move with your generation mix, and fuel burned outside the power sector, which falls as electrification rises.
  • 2Only part of the air responds. Windblown dust, open fires, agriculture and industrial process emissions do not move when the energy system changes, so that share is left alone.
  • 3Concentration becomes risk. A relative risk of 1.08 per 10 µg/m³, applied as exp(slope × change) − 1.
  • 4Risk becomes deaths. Colombia's measured burden of 15,741 deaths a year is rescaled by the attributable fraction at the new concentration against today's.
  • 5Where this stops working. Concentration is scaled in proportion to emissions — reasonable for moderate change, wrong for large change.
This is one possible result — what could make it higher or lower

No deaths are attributed below a counterfactual of 2.4–5.9 µg/m³, published as a range; carried through, that alone moves the result by roughly ±11% (the 260–323 in the tile). It is a parameter range, not a confidence interval. The dollar figure scales one-for-one with the value placed on an avoided death; the health effect does not.

Household air pollution — a second, separate burden

Driven by electrification of household energy, not by the generation mix. Colombia today: 6,283 deaths a year (WHO, 2021), against 15,741 from ambient exposure. Both scale with the SSP2 population path — 57.6 million Colombians by 2050.

Do not add this to the ambient figure above. The two burdens overlap — household combustion contributes to ambient concentrations. The WHO publishes the combined total with the overlap removed: 21,190 deaths (15,215–26,471), against a naive sum of 22,024. The overlap is 834 deaths, about 3.8% of the combined burden.
Local generator emissions are not modelled

These figures assume that added electricity replaces polluting cooking fuel in the home, which can reduce indoor smoke even if the supply is off-grid. A nearby diesel or petrol generator could add outdoor pollution that neither this household result nor the national power-mix calculation counts. The source of new household electricity in Colombia has not been measured here, so the combined health effect of off-grid supply is unresolved.
