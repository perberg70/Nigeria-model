A point is one hundredth of an energy-pollution index set to 1.00 for today's system; positive points reduce pollution. Components are rounded, so they sum to 4.2. Measured against the reference pathway's own 2050 level, energy-related air pollution is 3% lower.

How to read these numbers

Together the three effects produce a net change of +4.3 index points — 288 deaths a year avoided, the figure in the green tile. A high-electrification pathway delivers the strongest health gains when the additional electricity is also low-emission.

The largest driver is not on any slider you have moved: bioenergy is 2.5% of Colombia's generation but 73% of its power-sector fine particulates (Ember 2024 generation mix; EMEP/EEA 2023 Tier 1 emission factors), so which low-emission sources grow matters more here than how much low-emission generation there is. That choice sits in the optional source editor at step 2.

How these health figures are worked out
  • 1How much the emissions change. Two ratios against 2024: the power sector's particulate emissions, which move with your generation mix, and fuel burned outside the power sector, which falls as electrification rises.
  • 2Only part of the air responds. Windblown dust, open fires, agriculture and industrial process emissions do not move when the energy system changes, so that share is left alone. This step runs on 21.3 µg/m³ (McDuffie et al. 2021, 2017 base year), a different exposure estimate from the World Bank's 18.4 µg/m³ for 2023 shown below; the two do not reconcile. Industry fuel other than coal (9.1% of the concentration) is excluded because it mixes in cement and lime process emissions, so the effect of electrification may be understated by up to that much.
  • 3Concentration becomes risk. A relative risk of 1.08 per 10 µg/m³ (the value used in the TRA420 model; no epidemiological source is attached here), applied as exp(slope × change) − 1.
  • 4Risk becomes deaths. The WHO's estimated burden of 15,741 deaths a year (modelled attribution, not a count of death certificates) is rescaled by the attributable fraction at the new concentration against today's.
  • 5Where this stops working. Concentration is scaled in proportion to emissions — reasonable for moderate change, wrong for large change.
This is one possible result — what could make it higher or lower

Three published ranges move the result, and none is a confidence interval:

  • ±30%The size of the burden. The WHO's 15,741 is published as a range, and the result moves with it in proportion — roughly 200 to 370 avoided deaths.
  • ±11%Where clean air is defined. No deaths are attributed below a counterfactual of 2.4–5.9 µg/m³; carried through, that gives the 260–323 in the tile.
  • ±10%Which response function. McDuffie et al. (2021) report Colombia under two response functions — 11,818 deaths (GBD2019) and 14,390 (Global Exposure Mortality Model) — about ±10% around their mean.

The dollar figures scale one-for-one with the value per avoided death: US$1.67m in 2025 dollars, transferred from Viscusi & Masterman (2017, US$1.228m in 2015 dollars) and uprated by US inflation. The basis is not settled. OECD (2025, Table 6.1) gives group starting values of US$1.1m for low- and middle-income countries and US$7.1m for OECD members, in 2022 purchasing-power-parity dollars; uprated the same way they are US$1.21m and US$7.81m. Neither is OECD's recommended value for Colombia: its default is to scale the group value to the country's income, which this prototype has not done. The health effect itself does not depend on it.

Household air pollution — a second, separate burden

Driven by electrification of household energy, not by the generation mix. Colombia today: 6,283 deaths a year (WHO AIR_11, 2021), against 15,741 from ambient exposure. Both rise with population — 57.6 million Colombians by 2050 on the SSP2 path (IIASA-WiC POP 2025), 1.13× the 2021 population — and with energy-demand growth.

The WHO publishes the combined total with the overlap removed: 21,190 deaths (15,215–26,471), against a naive sum of 22,024. The overlap is 834 deaths, about 3.9% of that combined total.
Why these household figures lean high, and what they leave out
  • 1Household fuel grows too fast. It is scaled at the economy-wide income elasticity of 0.64, although residential energy grows more slowly and households burn less wood as they get richer. With electrification unchanged, that alone raises household deaths about 46% by 2050 — so the 2,952 avoided is measured against a reference that is likely too high.
  • 2The household mapping is not sourced. How economy-wide electrification carries into homes is an assumption (equal percentage points), not a measured relation.
  • 3The firewood share is dated. It comes from a 2015 carrier split; gas has displaced firewood since, so today's share is likely lower.
  • 4Deaths only. Illness that does not kill is left out, so as a measure of total health cost the figure is a floor.
Local generator emissions are not modelled

These figures assume that added electricity replaces polluting cooking fuel in the home, which can reduce indoor smoke even if the supply is off-grid. A nearby diesel or petrol generator could add outdoor pollution that neither this household result nor the national power-mix calculation counts. The source of new household electricity in Colombia has not been measured here, so the combined health effect of off-grid supply is unresolved.
