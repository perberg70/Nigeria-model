What runs the calculations

The integrated assessment model (IAM) is the calculation engine. It combines scenario data, climate modelling and economic relationships to work out the consequences of the choices you made.

  • SSPsThe scenarios you pick from. Five pathways, each pairing a socioeconomic storyline (SSP1 appears twice) with a forcing level in 2100; the starting assumption, not a result. Riahi et al. (2017) ↗
  • FaIRThe climate model. Takes emissions and works out temperature — a fast, simplified model calibrated against the large ones. Leach et al. (2021) ↗
  • DICEThe economy. Projects population, productivity and capital for each scenario — how large the economy is that the damage lands on. Barrage & Nordhaus (2024) ↗
  • DamageWarming into cost. The most contested step in the chain; the answer moves a lot with the curve used. Howard & Sterner (2025) ↗
This is one possible result — what could make it higher or lower

Every number on this screen is a single figure from one IAM parameter set. Read it as a pathway comparison, not a precise forecast. Three choices move it most:

  • ECSHow strongly the planet responds to CO₂ — set to 3 °C per doubling. Sherwood et al. give 2.6–3.9 °C as a 66% range and 2.3–4.7 °C as a 90% range. Sherwood et al. (2020) ↗
  • CurveHow damage grows with warming — different published curves give substantially different costs from identical warming. Moore et al. (2024) ↗
  • RateHow much the future counts — this tool uses a 0.5% pure time-preference rate; economists disagree about the right rate. Drupp et al. (2018) ↗
How the cost of carbon is worked out

One extra tonne of CO₂ is added in 2025, the climate model is run again, and the difference in damage over the following 450 years is added up and discounted by the Ramsey rule (pure time preference 0.5%, elasticity 1). Under SSP2-4.5 this gives US$718 per tonne in 2025, in PPP US$ of 2025.

That is high against the range most often quoted in policy: the damage curve used is far steeper than the classic quadratic one, and 0.5% is a low time-preference rate. Both are stated choices.

What the three lines are, and how this chart is computed

Colombia's national profile is anchored at 92.7 Mt of fossil-fuel and industrial carbon dioxide in 2024 and 60.3 Mt in 2005. The dashed grey line is the median of 6 Colombia SSP curves. Each uses Colombian population and GDP per capita, a paired IIASA Latin America (R5) final-energy factor, and that R5 run's indexed fossil/industrial CO₂ after adding back reported biomass CCS. The pale grey band spans the full available model range.

This is an SSP-scaled prototype baseline, not a calibrated or forcing-consistent Colombian forecast. Some adjusted model values remain negative; the available rows do not explain every removal or establish one for Colombia. This chart does not respond to the energy sliders. The green line reaches the 2050 mitigation target you set at step 2 and continues at the same proportional rate; it does not estimate a technology mix or biomass CCS. Land-use emissions and removals are excluded.

How this curve is computed

Ember reports about 26.6 Mt life-cycle CO₂e from 89.14 TWh of Colombian generation in 2024. The dashed curve uses national SSP population and income, a normalized regional energy and electricity path, and a regional mix change indexed to Ember's Colombian mix. It is the median of 6 model runs. The range across those runs, in the data table below, is not a probability interval or a published Colombia forecast.

Which sources make up this grid, and how to change them

The low-emission slider at step 2 fixes the size of each group; the optional source editor sets the split within a group. Wind set to 40% of a 30% low-emission share is 12% of all electricity. Every source carries its own life-cycle factor — bioenergy at 216 g/kWh is not the same "clean" as hydro at 24. Open the source editor at step 2
